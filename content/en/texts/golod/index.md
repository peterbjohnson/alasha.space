Golod means hunger in Russian. I can’t just say hungry or hunger in English. In one of my native languages, we say “Men ashpyn”, I am hungry but even that is grammatically incorrect. Hunger is a state of being. Hunger is the pain in your body and in your soul. We can’t say hunger as a noun even though the word for it exists – “ash”. But the first thing I think when I think of hunger as a noun in my native language is famine, Asharshylyq. We spell it in with a capital A, there can’t be famine with a small “a” because Asharshylyq is a historical and incredibly traumatic event in lives of many Kazakhs and Kazakhstanis, people who live in Kazakhstan. Pronouncing or writing down famine with a small “f” or a small “a” would downplay and even erase the tremendous effects this disaster had on thousands of lives. Both living and dead. The capital “A” does not only announce the tragedy, make it more visible, felt and present even in today’s discourse but also makes the victims and their lost bodies visible and alive in the narrative, in the language even if it is often pronounced in whispers. Asharshylyq happened, it was real, violent, and deadly. It was not just a historical “event” but an ongoing presence of the trauma we still find words to describe, understand and make sense of.

Some of my compatriots also borrow Ukrainian Holodomor – the famine of 1933. But that’s not how our ancestors called what happened to them, they did not speak Ukrainian in 1927, 1929, 1930 or 1932 and 1933 or even now. Holodomor is not part of our initial vocabulary. We borrow it from contemporary Ukrainian political memory. My ancestors called the same thing Asharshylyq or often didn’t pronounce that word at all, they silenced it and tried to bury this pain in their bodies. Being hungry is not only a state of suffering and eventually dying, it is also a state of silencing and erasing one’s painful experience, a form of self-censoring. Maybe this is a reason we, the Kazakhstani citizens living in 2025, still find it hard to find words and concepts to describe what happened. The borrowed *Holodomor* term tries to fill in the hollow empty spaces not just in our vocabularies, but also in the places with the missing graves, with the missing records of the victims and survivors, and within the bodies of those who continue to live through the effects of this trauma for third, fourth and perhaps even fifth generation. My tongue finds it hard to say “famine” or “golod” without feeling some deep physical pain inside my body. My collarbone tenses when I type these words, my stomach pulsates with the pain that strives to reach the surface of my skin, under my shirt, break through the physical barrier of my body and the room I am sitting in and run free.

“Men ashpyn”. I am hungry for stories, white spots and untold histories. I am hungry for details and the truth. I am lost and in constant pain looking for unidentified graves and places where I can go to commemorate those who died almost hundred years ago. The erasure of their existence and their death continues to haunt me in my daily life. I remember my first days in school where I had to learn to spell my last name – the Godsent, Kudaibergen. It’s not just a namesake, it’s the name of my paternal grandfather, the only son from a huge family who survived the atrocity of the Big History, that didn’t bother with body counts, the history that did not archive the victims or map out their last mass graves. Were my ancestors even buried, did their bones find the last resort in the soil that they cherished so much and called home? Bones to soil is a sacred tradition in places where I come from. If the body is not buried, the soul will not find the road back to heaven and will forever remain stuck in between, wandering lost. Did they find their last home? Did they find their last peace, or did they forever remain hungry for the lost paths and traces of where they were supposed to go? We don’t know and it makes us ever hungrier. Biz ashpyz[^1], we are in the state of lingering pain that lives within our bodies and within the air we breeze.

This pain keeps my father up at night. He tells my mom that in his nightmares foxes came and demanded fresh meat. “It’s time to go to the graveyard and make a prayer,” he says. A prayer to keep the lost souls at peace. Even for a moment. To tell the spirits of ancestors that we still remember them even if we don’t know where they lay silent. Even if we don’t know if their bones were thrown into a collective grave or were scattered across the steppe by wild animals.

Mountains of corpses. Nightmarish silence is their last song of departure. Or maybe the disparate shots heard from the distance from the makeshift death squads killing the last survivors in the village, aul, that loses its last traces of history and identity on its way to become a Soviet kolkhoz. Everything is collective now – the kettle that was seized from the hungry nomads, the land that was occupied by the blood-thirsty ideology, the trees that silently watched over inexplicable violence, the soil that hungrily absorbed blood, tears and last dying echoes of “unnecessary people” – ненужные люди.

The collective farm was renamed to the kolkhoz “Red Star” – winners dictate the topography. Mass graves were raised to the ground and forcefully erased from the memories of the new Soviet people who became the survivors and now remained hostages of the new system. “You are ordered to smile and praise the Soviet power! The Soviets who brought you freedom and class equality” but the same system that stole your livelihood, killed your parents and your sisters. Little girls and children were the weakest link for the Great Famine. Asharshylyq. Their tiny bodies were sold and exchanged for little food that was available. Big families sacrificed their female descendants to make soup out of their little bodies and feed to those they wanted to survive. Boys retain the bloodlines better. They are the heirs. They are the survivors. The miracles. The Godsents.

Kudaibergen is the name of my grandfather. He was a teenager at the time of famine. The miracle son born to an ageing father after numerous “failed” attempts. Maybe there were older sisters before him who were called “Let there be a boy!” – Ulbolsyn. Maybe not. We do not know. He and his mother were the only survivors of what once was a big family.

Saved from a makeshift Soviet gulag, they ran to the border not even once looking back. Maybe at the border they would escape from the system that aimed to exterminate them – the unnecessary people, the backward nomads, the unknown indigenous whose blood flows in the rivers they pass. His mother remembered these bloodied rivers till her death but only whispered about them to my father. Another miracle son, “Turarbek” – maybe he will stand up.

Born premature and in the family where six kids before him died in their cradles, he was the first surviving boy to the father who was the only surviving heir to his bloodline. Maybe he will stand up is less reassuring as Let There Be A Boy. There is no exclamation mark at the end of my father’s name, no proclamation, just a sign of an almost failed hope. Kazakh language has this interesting grammar with conditional turns – Turar? Will he stand? Will he survive? Or *Maybe, he will survive*. Turarbek.

The loss of hope runs in the family. It’s the feeling the survivors shared when they rushed out of the Soviet borders but were stopped short in the borderland. Just one mountain away from East Turkestan (in colonial, Xinjiang). Maybe this is where the feeling of lost hope or conditional hope with a question mark at the end of their names appeared. Was he a Godsent? Will he survive? So they passed it to their first heir who is given the hopeless name “will he stand up?”. Will we all survive? reads in my father’s name. But against the odds he did.

Miracle child again. And a first surviving boy! Malnourished, weak, but a boy. Kudaibergen took him to the Soviet passport bureau 2 months later as if trying to trick the system and trick the destiny by announcing that he was *not* prematurely born[^2] at 7 months but brought up to full term, so signed him up as born in January when he was supposed to, and not in October, when he did. The namesake remained the same but with a more affirmative “He might survive”. Still no exclamation mark at the end. In family name, he is not Sadykov – the descendant of the childless cousin, the name all of my relatives had to take to avoid Soviet repressions if they took the name of their repressed grandfather – Uksikbai. The “bai” in his name is the target for his violent repression – he was rich and Soviets did not like the rich. So he had to be dispossessed from his riches, his family killed almost completely (except for the Godsent miracle child Kudaibergen) and his grave must never be found.

My father, the second miracle child in the family line was born to break this unfair violence. So, he got the last name of his father – the son of Kudaibergen. Still Sovietized, with the “-ov” at the end of his last name and the patronymic the same – Kudaibergenov Turarbek Kudaibergenovich.

Pronounced twice – the son of the son of, truly the son of Kudaibergen.

My grandfather was tired of hiding or maybe he sensed that Stalin was about to die. The real enemy of the people.

The cycle of dispossession had to stop there.

Traditions of giving your children names of ancestors and your name as their last name was restored. If only the miracle child survives.

My father went through stages of liking and disliking his name. Tall and thin, he looked hungry on his teenage photos imitating the Beatles’ haircut and sometimes appearing with a pair of black sunglasses. The cool guy. The cool guy cannot be called “He might survive” so-and-so from the kolkhoz of the Red Star. His friends called him “John”. Maybe because he had bad eyesight and wore glasses and looked a bit like John Lennon (he would love that story) or maybe because he loved reading Western novels (the explanation he always gives). The lone and strange addition to the family of survivors, the cool guy in sunglasses. The only son of Kudaibergen.

Godsent meets He survived! The bloodline continues.

Flashback to my childhood. I sit meandering in my mind. It’s time to fill in the kundelik – the physical copy of my existence within the educational system of my country. I don’t have a passport yet, my photo gets sticked into my mother’s passport when we travel abroad. The only official sign of my existence in this country is my school “diary” but that’s not what kundelik is. It’s a personal record of my grades and my teaching schedule. It should have my name on the title page. I stubbornly spell it in Qazaq transliteration with all the “harsh” dybys, sounds of my native language. Dybys is the sound of the letter, phonetics I believe.

Soft “K” turns into “Q” scratching at the back of your throat and leaving waves of harsh sounds in your stomach where dybys, the sound travels after you harden it with the vowel – uuuuu, but not the soft Russian “у”, the Qazaq UUUUUh. Ұ.

Quuuuh-Daaaai-Bergen. Bergen runs quickly, staccato when the echoes of the harsh sounds Qudai (God) still vibrate in your throat, melt at the back of your tongue, stick to the sides of your stomach and continue further down until the depth of intestines mute it. Kazakh language distinguishes between harsh (zhuan) and soft (zhinishke) sounds sometimes with a variation of deep throat sounds or deep nasal sounds. Back vowels and front vowels. Scratchy sounds and soft ephemeral sounds. Yummy sounds and hungry sounds. Liquids, glides and fricatives. My all-time favourites are nasal sounds though these are the hardest ones to get lost in. My friend loves the deep throat ones. You announce a name, a word, a person in Qazaq by singing their name at the back of your throat or rolling the sound in the depth of your nose. Breath in, breath out, the body regulates the tone, the meaning, the feeling. I stretch Quuuuh-Daaaai- as if threading it from the top of my head to the last nerves of my toes and then run quickly to “bergen”. And then I eat the ending “-ova” because it is like a bad sauce that spoils the delicacy. It’s not necessary. My body spits it back like an old gum that lost all its tastes.

I want to give my name all the sounds and magic that it represents when I write it down in my school diary. But the truth is I don’t like it. I anxiously walk to my mom and ask her if I can write down her maiden name on the title page. My father is furious. They are not divorced yet to start the conversation of maiden names. “You are Kudaibergenova!” he yells angrily without stretching different parts of the namesake. “It’s our family name and the memory of our ancestors!”. I stand there perplexed in the centre of our living room. “Who cares?!” I finally say angrily. My horrid stubbornness plays a bad joke on me.

After that fight my father started having more nightmares. The foxes were demanding more bodies. The family album with old black and white pictures suddenly resurfaced on a dining table. In between greasy rice (mom made plov every Thursday “to spill the oil” in memory of ancestors and to feed the aruakhs, the spirits of the dead) and опьяняющие запахом лепешки (the ritualistic “Seven breads” were made to feed the spirits as well), I was handed small pictures of my father’s relatives. The history lesson began anew.

From seven breads (Zheti nan) came Seven ancestors (Zheti Ata). Sacredness spilled all over the dining table. The hunger pinched the lining of my stomach. The annoyance pinched the back of my eyes. Father’s voice lovingly wrapped my tiny teenage body with tenderness.

Fast-forward twenty-something years. Father and I are driving with windows open in the heat of early August. The huge green trees that embroider the roads of the old aul once named after the Red Star, once a kolkhoz. I stuck my head out and breathe this thick fat air full of familiar smells I knew since my childhood. Someone is burning the wood. Probably to grill the greasy mutton meat for lunch. The cows moan slowly at the distance. Dogs bark ready to jump under the wheels of our cars. We glance over the hills my great grandfather once called home. His bones are shattered somewhere nearby, but we don’t know where.

The son who survived (and should have been renamed from Turarbek to Turdybek) opens to me about the story I never heard before. The story of death, the story of famine. His voice is softer, sad. Not like he recently corrected my Kazakh from “qqqaarynym ashty” to “Men ashpyn” and laughed. Grammatically I’m incorrect. Today there’s no laughing. We talk quietly. Did you get the water bottle? Take a picture of this. We will stop there.

I just came back from my pilgrimage to Mangystau’s sacred places. Do we have relatives in Mangystau? I ask the son who survived. Finally, he laughs. No, we don’t. Did you understand the dialect out there? Did your Kazakh improve there? Yes, sir! I even went to a baqsy[^3] from Mongolia. She spoke to me only in Kazakh. All these silly things, janym. I believe in all of this magic, you know, I stubbornly tell him, and he laughs again.

We pass the places I remember since my childhood. Places that were not made for laughing. Only for silent remembrance and deep sorrow. But today is good. We bought red roses to place on the graves of my grandmothers. We divided them carefully into two bouquets and their опьяняющий smell fills in the insides of the car.

The old cemetery is dusty and dry. Be careful not to dirty your shoes, he says in Kazakh. It’s the language we share. With bits of Russian, though he still makes mistakes in gendered endings – gender is alien in Kazakh language; with fat chunks of my father’s tongue, ake tili[^4]. We made up this language from our constant road trips when he used to buy me bunch of chocolate candies for the long rides to the villages and I got to choose cassettes with foreign music. I was four, five, seven, six, nine. We barely spoke English but we would play this game when we guessed the meaning of the song by the sounds, dybys. Is it a sad sound? “I’m so hungry….” father would sing almost howling breaking into a loud laughter, and I’d choke on my giggles. Take my “Sneakers” bar if you’re hungry. He’d happily bite it. MMMM, nuts! Father loves nuts of all sorts. I know it, he knows it. We laugh again until we reach a village and have a greasy tasty horse meat soup. So good. Et. Meat. My whole Kazakh vocabulary at that time circled around food items. Zhylqy[^5] was my favourite word growing up. Zhylqy et. There’s something magical in it. Zhylqy is how I think of the hills in my father’s native aul. The hills where tons of horses ate the grass on the hills. Horses and Zhylqy et that belonged to my distant relatives. Zhylqy et that could have saved them from Asharshylyq. Dispossession takes a different twist when I think of it.

We do our ritual at the cemetery. Something called me here once I came back from Mangystau, the sacred place. The magic place. I brought the headscarf they gave me in one of the sufi shrines. Take it. But I can’t. Take it. And then drink it, they handed me a steel mug with murky salty water. I closed my eyes, and the warm water sipped through my throat. It’s sacred, they said. Strange dialect but my heart can follow it.

People stare at me at the shrine. I stick out with my bright green eyes and skin that’s too fair for the scorching sun out there. “You must not be from here?” a woman next to me says in this salty dialect. I swallow every sound like a delicious, cured delicacy, sur et[^6]. No, I’m not. I’m from the valley. Oh, water must taste sweet there.

Father hands me the bottle of cold spring water from the mountains from where we are from as I tell him this story and laughs. “You should have said our water tastes better here!”

He then closes his eyes. The hot water from samovar was what probably killed my grandfather. See, he loved the extra-hot tea too much. Bright, red tea. Qara shai[^7]. “No pessssok[^8] in it?” I playfully bring the Mangystau dialect to our conversation. We laugh but the water is sweeter *in here*.

My stomach is empty, so the water lands with echoes, jumping from one side to another just the same way when I pronounce the long “Qqqquuuudai-bergen” when I stare at my grandfather’s silent grave. What a coincidence, we are both Tauruses! The fancy old gentleman who stared at me from the black and white photos in the family album (wearing not one but two watches! What a show off!) suddenly becomes more familiar. I place a bit of dust in my hand and spread it across his grave. We are here and we remember. Deep down inside I also say, “thank you”. Thank you for my name, thank you for my green eyes that I inherited from you, thank you for my father.

Even though the cemetery is solemn, I am filled with joy. It must be the spirituality I brought from my trip to magical Mangystau. We place the fresh roses at the graves of my grandmothers.

So many burials but something is missing. I feel the emptiness within me that creeps into the space and runs into the village up the hills. So many more bodies and memories forever erased from this topography. My body aches with these unexplained tickles as if invisible ants run through my arms biting every millimetre of my skin from within. And a strange feeling of hunger fills me in on the way back home. I almost never feel hunger anymore. Strange thing I managed to develop. Genetically modify myself to fit in better to current beauty standards.

But *here*, in these picturesque green valleys with hills I *always* feel hungry.

[^1]: We are hungry, Kaz.

[^2]: Kazakhs believe in the sacredness of language so I cannot write “was not born prematurely” because it creates a sense that one “was not born” at all. My brain thinks in Kazakh as I write this and in Kazakh the connection “not” would be separated from the verb “born” by several words in a sentence and the negating term “emes” will be placed right at the end of the sentence. One can question, why don’t *you* write this text in Kazakh? Qazaq tili, the Kazakh language is a spirit, aruakh, that lives in my body but my skill of writing in Kazakh is probably lost. Or so I’m told. I left Kazakh school decades ago but continued to write in this language till my thirties. I wrote my most secret thoughts in Kazakh when I feared foreign secret service officers will seize my field notes. They would not know my native language. I’d sit still in their interrogation rooms, my lips sealed. This feeling of mental torture still lives in my body silencing me. What can’t *you* write this text in Russian? Can I write about colonial traumas in the language of the colonizer? Not the people (I’m no Russophobe), but the system (my fingers still shivered when I read old KGB documents in Russian the other day).

[^3]: Female shaman.

[^4]: The allegory for “ana tili” - mother tongue.

[^5]: Specific name for the horse meat in Kazakh. Kazakhs eat horse meat both as traditional delicacy and a sacred dish of survival and healing.

[^6]: Dried up horse meat, a delicacy.

[^7]: Black tea, kaz. Black tea means it is not with milk. Milky tea is traditional in Kazakh villages. Some pots are made already with milk in it and people in the villages were surprised for the longest time I could remember that both I and my father preferred non-milky tea, qara shai, the simplest tea. Milky tea is considered a sign of honour to the guests because milk is nourishing and often expensive (especially if villagers do not own their own cow). Every element of food that is offered at the dining table in a Kazakh household has a sacred and symbolic meaning like horse meat and different parts of bones to grind, like the fat milky tea. When I write it now, I also think that this symbolism has some traces of memories of famine and post-famine that required nourishing foods for the survivors to live on both physically and mentally.

[^8]: In some Kazakh dialect, the word “sugar” (qant in Kazakh) took on a different term and became a Russian word “sand” – pesok but with a specific, localised sounds where sss is prolonged and stuck between the tongue and teeth like a slip. Pesssok. When I travel outside my region in Kazakhstan, I love picking up these differences in words and pronunciations especially where colonial language is transformed and localised in the most unexpected locations like “sand” for “sugar”.
